Same frame.Then compare.

How to take before and after photos for home repairs that are actually easy to compare: lock four visual anchors before the work begins, then repeat them during progress and recheck photos.

BEFORE · FRAME 01
RECHECK · FRAME 01
A photo is a record.

It is not proof that a repair is complete, safe or well made. The useful part is being able to see the same place from the same viewpoint later.

Why comparisons fail

The “after” can look better for the wrong reason.

A different angle hides the old condition. A closer crop removes context. Brighter daylight changes the whole scene. Build the comparison before anyone starts work.

01

The camera drifts

The after photo is a metre to the left or held at chest height instead of eye height. Choose a standing spot and one fixed edge in the frame.

02

The scale disappears

A tight detail may show texture but not location or size. Take one wide orientation photo and one repeatable detail.

03

The story stops too early

“Finished” is reported, but nobody photographs the same spot again later. Add a recheck frame that records what is visible—not what was promised.

Interactive practice

Build the shot card before lifting the camera.

Pick a project type and a capture stage. The card changes its stance anchor, overview, detail and reference shot. Nothing is uploaded or saved.

Floor or threshold · Before work

Lock the doorway, not just the floor.

Stand where both door-frame edges are visible. That frame gives the later photo a repeatable boundary and keeps the floor change in context.

1 · Stance anchorMark the camera spot by a door-frame corner or fixed tile seam.
2 · Wide frameInclude both sides of the opening and the full walking line.
3 · Detail frameMove closer without changing orientation; include one adjacent fixed edge.
4 · Reference framePlace a ruler beside the condition only if it is safe and does not cover it.

The four anchors

Repeat these; ignore the fancy gear.

Compare two repair photos on this device with a sliding divider or inspect each frame on its own.

Consistency beats camera quality. A phone photo can be useful when its viewpoint is repeatable and the frame carries enough context.

1

Standing spot

Name a tile seam, door edge, step or other fixed place where the camera is held.

2

Camera height

Eye, chest or waist height. Pick one and record it in plain words.

3

Frame edges

Put two fixed objects on opposite edges so drift is obvious later.

4

Light note

Daylight, room lights or flash. Try to repeat it; never claim a change the lighting created.

Before anyone starts

  • Take one wide orientation photo and one detail.
  • Keep faces, mail, keys, screens and personal papers out of frame.
  • Write the stance, camera height and frame-edge cues.
  • If a scale reference is useful, place it without touching an unsafe area.

When someone says “done”

  • Repeat the wide frame before taking flattering close-ups.
  • Photograph what is visible; do not label hidden work as verified.
  • Record who reported completion separately from your observation.
  • Recheck later from the same anchor when appropriate.
Stop at documentation. Electrical, gas, structural, fire-safety and mobility concerns belong with qualified professionals. A matching photo can support a conversation; it cannot certify the work.
