Let the person who lives here choose one change

Before a holiday visit turns into a room-by-room project, ask whether your host wants help with anything. A familiar arrangement may work well for them. If they would rather just visit, put the card away.

Download the fillable visit card (PDF)

Write on paper or type into five fields in a PDF reader. Keep the card with your host, and ask before sharing it.

Use the five questions without downloading
  1. What already works for you here?
  2. What would you like to change, if anything?
  3. What small next step feels right?
  4. Who, if anyone, has your invitation to help?
  5. Would you like a follow-up? If so, when and how?

Example. “Keep the bench where it is. I want to try a bowl for my gloves on the shelf.” Jo offers to bring a spare bowl on Sunday. Their host asks Jo to check back on Friday.

Scene one · Saturday at Mum’s, made up but familiar

10:05 amYou arrive with coffee and no plan beyond “have a look round”. The rug in the hall has a curled corner. You have seen it for six years.

10:20 amPhone out. Hallway: loose rug, cord across the path to the lamp. Two taps each, a photo, a marker on the exact spot. This week.

10:50 amBathroom walkthrough. The question is whether she has any concern about stepping into the bath; she says no, then says “well”. Grab bar: this month, and a note to ask about an occupational therapist visit.

12:30 pmNine rooms, eleven items, four of them done before lunch with tape, a bulb and a nightlight. The rest go out as a plain-text list to your sister with names next to them.

A month laterA reminder: look again at the hall rug. It has crept back. You fix it properly this time.

Scene two · how the weekend works
1

Walk every room with a list

The CDC’s Check for Safety has 16 questions across stairs, floors, kitchen, bedrooms and bathrooms. Ask them out loud, with your parent.

2

Sort by effort, not by room

This week: tape, bulbs, nightlights, a bath mat. This month: handrails, grab bars, stair lighting, a proper step stool. Someday: furniture and shelves.

3

Put names on it, then look again

A list with owners and dates gets done; a list without them is a wish. Recheck in 30 days, because rugs come back.

Scene four · questions
Where do I start when making a parent’s house safer?

With the CDC checklist above, room by room, on a day your parent is home and part of it. The this-week items are cheap and quick (rugs, cords, bulbs, nightlights, a bath mat); the this-month items need a handyman or electrician (handrails, grab bars, stair lighting). Do the first group the same day.

Should I hire a professional?

For mobility, structural, electrical or fire-safety concerns, yes. An occupational therapist can do a home safety assessment tailored to how your parent actually moves; ask their doctor or the local Area Agency on Aging. RoomMend’s own listing says it is not an inspection, certification or fall-risk assessment.

How do I keep the fixes from slipping back?

Recheck. A rug comes back, a bulb goes again. RoomMend plans a reminder to look again 30 days after a fix is ticked done, and keeps before, after and recheck photos in the room’s history, per the listing.

How do I get my siblings to actually help?

Give them a list with names on it. The app sends the fix list by Messages or email as plain text, or a PDF per room, and each fix can carry who is doing it and a target date. The copy box above makes a plain-text version right now.

No address, no location, no account, no ads, no analytics; records and photos stay on the device and it works offline, per the listing. Optional Face ID lock.
