What to do when your kid refuses math worksheetsFive-minute games, no paper, by grade.

Brainy Bucks · One free quest every day

Try the same maths in a different format. If a subtraction page stalls at 12 − 4, lay out twelve counters, move four away and ask how many remain. Watching which step needs help gives you something specific to discuss with the teacher.

Five minutes, no paper

Read three picture graphs and use each key to solve six questions about totals and differences.

KindergartenSock count+

Start with six socks. Ask the child to make pairs. Count together: three pairs, six socks. Add a seventh sock and ask what stays unpaired.

Grade 1Number line hopscotch+

Mark 0 to 20 on a floor or sheet of paper. Start at 7 and hop 5 spaces forward to 12. Hop 4 back to 8. If the count is off by one, touch 7 first and count only the hops.

Grade 2Kitchen shop+

Pretend an apple costs 25 cents and a cracker costs 15 cents. Buy both with a dollar: 40 cents spent, 60 cents left. Show the change with coins or ten-cent counters. Switch roles and try new prices.

Grade 3Times-table tennis+

Ask, “What is 4 × 6?” Arrange four rows of six objects and count 24. Switch roles: the child asks 6 × 4. Compare the rows and talk about why the total stayed the same.

Grade 4Fraction pizza+

Draw a circle divided into eight equal slices. Cover three and ask what fraction is still visible: five eighths. Is three eighths less than a half? Yes: half is four of the eight slices. Try the same questions with a rectangle.

Who this is not for

  • A child with a diagnosed maths learning difficulty who has a specialist plan. Follow the plan; these games can sit beside it, not instead of it.
  • For assigned homework, ask the teacher which steps they need to see on paper.

Questions parents ask

Why does my child refuse math worksheets but play math games happily?

Ask which part feels hard: reading the instructions, choosing an operation, doing the calculation or writing the answer. Try one problem aloud with objects, then try the same numbers on paper. Notice which step changes.

Is five minutes a day enough?

Five minutes gives you time to work through one example and ask the child to explain a step. Choose the next activity based on what you see. The time alone cannot tell you whether the child understands the skill.

Should I just stop the worksheets?

For assigned work, ask the teacher which skill the worksheet practises and whether another format is acceptable. For practice you chose at home, try one of the games and compare how the child approaches the same problem.
