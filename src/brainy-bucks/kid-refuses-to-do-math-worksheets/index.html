You know the scene. The worksheet comes out of the book bag, the pencil goes down, the chair tips back and a child who was fine thirty seconds ago is now a child who cannot possibly, ever, do twenty subtraction problems. It happens in kitchens everywhere, and it is almost never about subtraction.

A worksheet is a long row of chances to be wrong with no reaction in between, done sitting still, usually at the worst hour of the day. Most of what a seven-year-old needs from practice is the opposite: one question at a time, something in their hands, a quick answer back, and a stop before it stops being fun. The research guide for children who struggle with maths, from the US Department of Education's What Works Clearinghouse, lists six things with strong evidence; four of them (concrete objects, number lines, deliberate word problems and short timed activities) fit in a kitchen better than on a page.

Questions parents ask

Why does my child refuse math worksheets but play math games happily?

A worksheet is twenty chances to be wrong in a row with nothing between them. A game is one question at a time with a reaction after each. Children are not avoiding the maths; they are avoiding the format. The games above keep the maths and change the format, which is also what Brainy Bucks does with a wrong answer: a clue and a second try, no red X and no lives lost.

Is five minutes a day enough?

For practice, short and daily beats long and weekly for most children, and it is what a child will actually agree to. The research guide for struggling learners recommends regular timed activities as one way to build fluency, alongside number lines, concrete objects and explicit work on word problems. Five minutes with one of those is not nothing. It is most of the list.

Should I just stop the worksheets?

If a teacher sends them home, talk to the teacher before you bin them; most will happily accept a photo of a sock-count or a shop game instead for a while. If the worksheets are yours, yes, stop. The goal is practice that happens, not a page that gets completed in tears.
