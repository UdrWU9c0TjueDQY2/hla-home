Questions parents ask the night the lights go out

When should a family leave the house in a winter storm?

When the house cannot be kept safely warm, when someone depends on a powered medical device and the backup is running out, or when officials tell you to. Roads during the storm are their own danger, so the decision is usually made early (before the worst of it) or late (after plows run), not in the middle. The chart below walks through it.

Can we run the generator in the garage with the door open?

No. The CDC says a generator, charcoal grill or camp stove must be outside and at least 20 feet from any window, door or vent, never in a home, basement or garage (CDC winter safety release, February 2021). Carbon monoxide is the storm death that happens indoors.

Can we heat the kitchen with the oven?

No. The same CDC guidance: never heat the house with a gas oven and never burn anything in a stove or fireplace that is not vented.

What goes on the Emergency Card for a child?

Who to call first, allergies, current medications and important health information, in plain words a stranger could read. Family Ready saves it on the phone so it opens in Emergency Mode without internet once the phone is unlocked, and you can make one for each child, a parent or someone you care for, with their permission.

Does the app call 911 for us?

No. Family Ready never calls or messages anyone automatically; in Emergency Mode you tap a contact to start the call yourself. In a life-threatening emergency, call your local emergency number first.

What if the phone dies?

Then the app is a brick, which is why Family Ready lets you print a dated Emergency Snapshot PDF for free and why its own listing says to keep a printed copy. Tape one inside a kitchen cupboard and put one in the car.

Drag the kit into your family's order

Six things from the Ready.gov-style kit checklist that Family Ready ships with. Drag (or use the arrow keys) to put them in the order you would grab them. There is no single right order; the point is that you have thought about it before the lights go out.

A Thursday in January

7:10 pm, ice storm, the power cuts mid-dinner. The kids think it is fun for about four minutes. The house is 68°F and will lose roughly a degree an hour; the forecast says restoration by morning, maybe.

The chart says stay: no medical devices, roads are glass, the gas fireplace is vented. The kit is in the hall cupboard. Emergency Mode on the phone shows the Emergency Card for each child, with the paediatrician and grandma's number, and the printed snapshot is taped inside the cupboard door in case the phone goes. Everyone sleeps in the living room under three blankets each. The lights come back at 5 am. Nobody had to decide anything in the dark.

What the free parts look like

Three things to do this week

  1. Fill one Emergency Card per child. Who to call first, allergies, medications, health notes. It is free, saved on the phone, and opens offline in Emergency Mode once the phone is unlocked.
  2. Print the Emergency Snapshot. Free, dated PDF. Cupboard door and glovebox. The app's listing says it itself: no app helps if the phone is lost or out of battery.
  3. Edit the kit checklist and set a review date. The checklist is based on Ready.gov and Get Prepared (Canada) guidance with optional sections for children, older adults, pets and rural homes. Choose a 3, 6 or 12-month review so it does not go stale.

Who should pick something else

  • If you want an app that alerts you to storms or calls for help, this is not it; Family Ready organizes information you enter and never contacts emergency services. Use your local alerts app alongside it.
  • If you want remote family sync and printable packs for a babysitter or carer, those are part of Family Ready Plus, a subscription. The free tier is the card, Emergency Mode, the household plan, the kit checklist, encrypted backup and the snapshot PDF.
  • If nobody in the household will keep a phone charged, make the paper version the plan and the app the backup.
